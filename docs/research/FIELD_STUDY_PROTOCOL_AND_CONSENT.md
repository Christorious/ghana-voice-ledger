# Field Study Protocol: Trader-Customer Interaction and Spoken Sales in Ghanaian Markets

Draft protocol for ethics review and field use, with consent instruments. Version 0.1, 30 September 2026. Fields in square brackets are to be completed before submission. Nothing in this document has yet been reviewed by an ethics committee or by the Data Protection Commission; translations into Twi, Ga and Ewe must be prepared by a qualified linguist and back-translated before use.

## Part I. Protocol

### 1. Title and summary

Title: How sales are spoken: an observational and elicitation study of trader-customer interaction in Ghanaian open markets, in support of an open, non-commercial voice bookkeeping tool.

Principal investigator: [name, affiliation, email, phone]. Supervisor or academic sponsor: [name]. Ethics committee: [University of Ghana Ethics Committee for the Humanities, or KNUST Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics, or Ashesi University review]. Data controller under the Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843): [institution or investigator], Data Protection Commission registration number [pending].

Summary. Market traders in Ghana rarely keep written records, and a voice tool that lets a trader speak a sale in Twi, Ga or Ewe could change that. Building such a tool requires knowing what a sale sounds like, and no study has ever treated the Ghanaian market transaction as a unit of analysis. This study observes and, with consent, records real trader-customer transactions at three markets, interviews traders and buyers, elicits how prices, quantities and credit are spoken in each language, and tests how traders naturally phrase a sale when asked to say it to a device. Its outputs are transaction-level statistics that do not yet exist for Ghana, an annotation scheme, a lexicon of numerals, units and money terms, and a small consented corpus. The study is non-commercial; its tool will be free and open source, and its anonymised results and any openly released data will be published under a Creative Commons licence.

### 2. Background and rationale

Roughly nine in ten Ghanaian businesses are informal and most keep no accounts. Half of the micro-owners in a Central Region study who kept no records cited fear of the authorities, and the causal literature shows that the record-keeping habits that last are the simplest ones. Speech technology for Ghanaian languages has advanced, yet the best open recogniser still transcribes general Twi speech with a word error near 45 percent, and the only field study of voice-based business record keeping in Africa, conducted in Nairobi in 2023, found that noise, code-switching, social norms and the timing of trade defeated a voice interface at the counter. Whether a voice bookkeeper can work in a Ghanaian market therefore turns on empirical facts nobody has measured: how long a sale takes, how many turns it has, which languages and numeral systems are used, how prices and quantities are bound to units such as the olonka or the heap, how often bargaining occurs, how often a sale closes in silence, how credit is spoken, and what a trader would say if asked to record a sale in her own words. Anthropological work in Kumasi and Accra and linguistic work on Akan market persuasion describe the texture of market talk but report no transaction statistics; Ga and Ewe market speech are undocumented. This study fills that gap and produces the first evaluation material for sale-event detection in Ghanaian languages.

### 3. Aims and research questions

The study aims to describe the structure of trader-customer transactions in Ghanaian markets across languages and commodity types, to quantify how much of a sale is expressed in speech, to document how numerals, units, money and credit are spoken in Twi, Ga, Ewe, Ghanaian English and Pidgin, and to learn how traders would naturally phrase a sale to a recording device.

The research questions are these. What phases and speech acts make up a market transaction, and how often does each occur? What is the distribution of transaction duration and turn count by commodity, customer type and site? Which languages, and which numeral systems, are used in each phase, and how often do speakers switch within a transaction? What share of the information a ledger needs, item, quantity, unit, unit price, total, payment mode and counterparty, is recoverable from speech alone, and what share only from observation? How are prices, units, mobile-money payments and credit verbalised, and what ambiguities arise, including the old-cedi habit of quoting in thousands? How do traders phrase a sale when asked to speak it for a record, and how consistent are these phrasings across traders and languages? What do traders and buyers think about being recorded at the stall, and under what conditions would they accept it?

### 4. Design

The study is a mixed-methods field study with five components run at each site over two half-day sessions per trader: structured observation of transactions with a coding sheet; consented audio recording of the trader's own speech and, where the customer agrees, of the exchange; short exit interviews with a subsample of buyers and a semi-structured interview with each trader; elicitation tasks in each language for numerals, units, prices and sale phrasings; and a Wizard-of-Oz test in which the trader speaks a sale to a phone while a facilitator plays the role of the system. The study is descriptive and generates no intervention; no product is deployed and no trader's records are changed.

### 5. Sites, languages and commodity strata

Three sites are proposed, with a fourth if funds allow. Makola or Kaneshie market in Accra covers the Ga, Twi, English and Pidgin mix of the capital. Kejetia market in Kumasi covers Asante Twi and permits comparison with earlier ethnographic and linguistic work there. Tema Community One market, or a market in Ho, covers Ewe alongside Ga. Tamale Central Market or the Techiman wholesale market would add Dagbani and Hausa or wholesale units as a fourth site. Within each site, four commodity strata separate the unit systems: vegetables sold by heap, such as tomato, pepper and onion; grains and flours sold by olonka or bowl, such as maize, gari and rice; fish sold by size or piece; and provisions or manufactured goods sold by piece, which serve as a fixed-price comparison. Each stratum is represented by at least two traders per site.

### 6. Participants, sampling and recruitment

Participants are of four kinds. Traders are adults who own or run a stall in one of the strata, recruited to a target of eight to twelve per site, forty in all, with attention to sex, age and whether they are migrants to the city. Stall assistants or apprentices who work at a participating stall are separate participants with their own consent. Customers are adults who buy at a participating stall during a session; their speech may be recorded only with their agreement, and a subsample of about five per trader is invited to a two-minute exit interview. Market leaders, including market managers, association secretaries and commodity queens, are consulted for permission and may be interviewed about norms.

Recruitment proceeds through the market's own structures. The investigator first meets the market management and the relevant commodity queen or association secretary with a letter of introduction from the institution and a one-page explainer in English and the local language, and asks for permission and for introductions. Traders are then approached at their stalls by a facilitator fluent in the language, given the explainer, and invited to a consent conversation at a time of their choosing. No trader is approached cold without the association's knowledge, since prior experience in West African markets shows vendors distrust unintroduced researchers. Traders under eighteen, and stalls where a minor is the only person present, are not recruited. Customers who appear to be minors are not interviewed, and transactions in which a minor is the customer are excluded from transcription and release and counted only in aggregate.

### 7. Procedures

Each participating trader is visited for two half-day sessions on different days, one covering the morning peak and one the afternoon, for about four hours each. A facilitator who speaks the trader's language sits within view of the stall but outside the customer's path. Before the first session, the trader completes the consent conversation, a short registration form and the elicitation tasks. At the start of each session the facilitator fits a wired lapel microphone to the trader's clothing, connected to a phone in the trader's pocket or a pouch recording continuously, and places a second phone as a boundary microphone at the stall, both recording uncompressed mono audio at 16 kHz or higher; the facilitator claps once in view of both devices to synchronise them and starts the coding sheet on a tablet. A bilingual sign is displayed at the stall. During the session the facilitator completes one row of the coding sheet per transaction in real time, noting the time from the tablet clock so that rows can be aligned to the audio, and marks any transaction whose customer declines recording or is a minor so that the corresponding audio can be deleted. After every fifth transaction, or when the stall is quiet, the facilitator invites the departing customer to a two-minute exit interview. At the end of the second session the facilitator conducts the trader interview and the Wizard-of-Oz test in a quieter spot nearby. Video is not used in this phase. Recording stops whenever the trader asks, and the trader may pause it at any time by pressing a button on the phone or by telling the facilitator.

The coding sheet is implemented in an open-source mobile data collection tool such as KoboToolbox or ODK Collect and records, per transaction, the start and end time; customer type as regular or new and apparent sex and age band; the languages used by each party in each phase; the address term used; whether a greeting occurred; the form of the price ask; the form of the first quote, including numeral system, currency word and unit; whether a reduction was requested and in what form; the number of offers and counter-offers; whether a dash was requested or given; the form of acceptance as verbal, quantity imperative or behavioural; quantity and unit; the number of items; payment mode as cash, mobile money, credit or mixed; whether change was discussed; the form of closing; the observed total; whether the facilitator judges the sale to have been completed; and which phases contained no speech.

The trader interview, of about thirty minutes, covers how the trader keeps track of sales and credit today, who owes her and how she remembers, how she uses mobile money, how she says prices and whether customers use old-cedi figures, what "last price" means at her stall, whether and when she bargains, how she would feel about a phone recording sales, from whom such a record should be private, whether she would want an assistant's sales recorded, what she would most like to know about her business at the end of a day, and what she would never allow a device to do with her money. The buyer exit interview, of about two minutes, asks whether the buyer is a regular, whether they bargained, which language they used and why, how they paid, whether they noticed the sign and how they feel about the recording, and whether they would want a spoken receipt.

The elicitation tasks, of about twenty minutes per trader and run in her language, ask the trader to say aloud a printed or spoken list of prices from fifty pesewas to ten thousand cedis in the way she would say them to a customer, then in the way an older customer might say them; to name, from photographs, the units she sells by and to say a price for each; and to speak a sale to the phone for ten scripted scenarios that cover cash, mobile money, credit, a dash, a multi-item basket, a partial payment, a query about who owes her, a return, a purchase from a supplier and an expense. The Wizard-of-Oz test then presents the phone as a bookkeeping assistant: the facilitator reads a short introduction, the trader says three real sales from the session in her own words, and the facilitator, acting as the system, reads each back and asks for confirmation, recording the trader's reaction and corrections.

### 8. Data management

Audio is recorded as uncompressed WAV, mono, 16 kHz or 48 kHz, on encrypted phones with no network sync. Each recording, coding row, interview and elicitation file is labelled with a site code, a trader pseudonym, a session number and a timestamp, and never with a name. The link between pseudonym and identity is kept in a single encrypted file held by the principal investigator and destroyed at the end of the study. Each evening the facilitator deletes audio segments flagged for deletion, backs up the day's files to an encrypted drive, and uploads them to the institution's secure storage; nothing is stored on personal cloud accounts or messaging services. Transcripts replace personal names, phone numbers and mobile-money identifiers with placeholders. Per-clip metadata follow the fields used by the University of Ghana speech corpus, namely speaker pseudonym, locale, sex, age band, device, environment and year, extended with the commodity stratum, the customer's consent status, and signal-to-noise and silence measures computed automatically. Transcription follows a written convention, with a verbatim tier that writes numbers as spoken and tags disfluency, laughter, unclear speech, noise and overlap, a normalised tier, per-token language tags for code-switching checked by an automatic lint, and a separate structured field carrying the numeric value of every amount; the checker is never the transcriber. Annotation labels phases and speech acts using ISO 24617-2 general-purpose functions plus a domain layer of bargaining acts and sale slots, with twenty percent double-annotated. Data are retained for [five] years after the study ends and then destroyed, except for the openly released subset described in section 11.

### 9. Ethical considerations

Risks to traders are loss of selling time, discomfort at being observed, and the possibility that a record of sales could reach someone the trader does not want to see it, such as a tax officer, a relative or a competitor. These are addressed by observing rather than interrupting trade, by paying for the time spent on interviews and elicitation, by storing only pseudonymised data, by never sharing individual sales data with anyone, and by reporting results only in aggregate. The study will not collect the trader's name on any data file, will not ask for her mobile-money number, and will not report any trader's takings. Risks to stall assistants are that a recording could be used by the owner as evidence about them; assistants therefore give their own consent, may decline without the owner being told the reason, and are assured in writing that no recording or transcript will be shared with the owner. Risks to customers are being recorded without wanting to be; these are addressed by the sign, by the facilitator's verbal request, by deletion on refusal, by exclusion of minors, and by the rule that customer speech is never released openly. Benefits are indirect: a clearer understanding of traders' own practices, a tool built to serve them, and findings returned to the market association. Compensation is set above the daily minimum wage without being an inducement: traders receive [GH¢60] per half-day session and [GH¢40] for the interview and elicitation session plus airtime for the phone calls the study requires, assistants receive [GH¢30] per session, buyers who complete the exit interview receive [GH¢5] of airtime, and market leaders who are interviewed receive [GH¢40]. Voice is personal data under the Data Protection Act 2012, so the data controller will be registered with the Data Protection Commission before collection begins, consent will be recorded, participants may withdraw at any time before release and have their data deleted, and the study will process no data beyond its stated purpose. Findings will be presented to each market association and to participating traders in their languages.

### 10. Analysis plan

Transaction-level statistics will be reported by site, commodity stratum and customer type: duration, turn count, phase presence rates, bargaining rate, number of offers, dash rate, acceptance type, payment mode, language mix by phase, numeral system by phase, and the share of phases without speech. The verbalisation ratio, defined as the number of ledger slots recoverable from the transcript alone divided by the number recoverable from transcript and observation together, will be reported with confidence intervals overall and by stratum, and is the study's primary outcome. Inter-annotator agreement will be reported as Cohen's kappa for phase and act labels and as exact-match agreement for slot values. Elicited price and unit utterances will be tabulated into a lexicon per language, with frequencies of each numeral system and currency phrasing and a catalogue of ambiguities. Wizard-of-Oz utterances will be clustered by structure to identify the canonical forms traders use spontaneously, and trader corrections to read-back will be classified. Interview data will be analysed thematically. The analysis plan and the coding sheet will be pre-registered on the Open Science Framework before fieldwork.

### 11. Outputs, data sharing and licence

The study will produce a report to the ethics committee and the market associations, a paper for a computational linguistics or information-and-communication-technologies-for-development venue, a public annotation scheme and lexicon under CC-BY-4.0, and a corpus in two tiers. The restricted tier, containing real transaction audio and full transcripts, will be available to researchers under a data-use agreement approved by the institution. The open tier, released under CC-BY-4.0, will contain only material whose speakers ticked the open-release box on their consent form: traders' elicited and Wizard-of-Oz utterances, transcripts with names removed, and aggregate statistics. Customer speech will not be included in the open tier under any circumstances. Any speech model trained on the restricted tier will be released under a licence that permits non-commercial use, and any model trained only on the open tier and on other openly licensed data may be released without restriction.

### 12. Team, timeline and budget

The team comprises the principal investigator; a field coordinator; two facilitators per site fluent in the site's languages, trained for two days in the coding sheet, consent procedure and equipment; one transcriber per language with a linguist consultant who writes the transcription convention and adjudicates disagreements; and a data manager responsible for encryption, backup and pseudonymisation. The timeline runs about fourteen weeks: weeks one to six for ethics submission, Data Protection Commission registration, translation and back-translation of instruments, permissions from market authorities, facilitator training and a one-day pilot at one stall; weeks seven to ten for fieldwork at three sites in sequence; weeks eight to thirteen for transcription and annotation in parallel; and week fourteen for analysis and reporting. The indicative budget, to be priced locally, covers participant compensation for forty traders, assistants, about two hundred buyers and leaders; facilitator and transcriber stipends; six lapel microphones, six phones or the use of team phones, two tablets, encrypted drives and printing; transport and per diems; ethics and registration fees; and translation. On the regional transcription rates reviewed for this project, about thirty to thirty-six labour-hours per audio hour should be assumed for careful transcription of two hours of audio per site.

### 13. Quality assurance and monitoring

The pilot day will test synchronisation, microphone placement, the coding sheet and the consent conversation, and will be followed by revisions. Facilitators will complete a five percent audit of their own coding rows against audio each evening. The field coordinator will review consent forms daily for completeness. Any adverse event, such as a complaint from a trader, customer or market authority, will be logged and reported to the ethics committee within [seven] days, and recording at the affected stall will stop until resolved.

## Part II. Instruments

### Appendix A. Transaction coding sheet fields

Each row holds: transaction identifier; start and end time; customer type (regular, new, unknown); customer apparent sex and age band (adult, older adult, minor); trader language by phase and customer language by phase, chosen from Twi, Ga, Ewe, English, Pidgin, Hausa, Dagbani, other, none; greeting present; address term used, free text; price-ask form (Twi, Ga, Ewe, English, Pidgin, none); first quote numeral system (Twi, Ga, Ewe, English, mixed); currency word used (cedi, Ghana, pesewa, old-cedi thousands, none); unit stated in quote (heap, olonka, bowl, size, piece, crate, other, none); reduction requested (yes, no) and form; number of seller offers; number of buyer counter-offers; "last price" uttered; dash requested; dash given; acceptance type (verbal, quantity imperative, behavioural, unclear); quantity and unit; number of distinct items; total observed; payment mode (cash, mobile money, credit, mixed); change discussed; closing form (thanks, blessing, none); phases with no speech, multi-select; consent status of customer (consented, declined, not asked, minor); notes.

### Appendix B. Trader interview guide

Opening. Thank you for allowing us to sit with you. I would like to ask about how you keep track of your business and how you talk with customers. There are no right answers, and you can skip any question.

Record keeping. How do you know at the end of the day how much you sold? Do you write anything down, and if so where? How do you remember who owes you? Has a customer's debt ever been forgotten or disputed? Do you use a susu collector, and does the collector keep a record for you?

Mobile money. How many of your customers pay by mobile money? Do you keep the messages? Does mobile money make it easier or harder to know your takings?

Prices and talk. How do you tell a customer the price? Do some customers still say prices in the old way, in thousands? What does "last price" mean at your stall? Which customers do you bargain with, and which not? Do you sometimes add something extra instead of reducing the price?

Recording. How did it feel to have the microphone today? If a phone could keep a record of your sales when you speak them, would you use it? Who should never be able to see such a record? If you have someone selling for you, would you want their sales recorded, and would they agree? What would you most want to know about your business each evening? What must a device never do with your money?

Closing. Is there anything about selling here that we have not asked and should know?

### Appendix C. Buyer exit interview

Good afternoon. We are studying how people buy and sell in this market. May I ask you five short questions? It will take two minutes and you can stop at any time. Do you buy from this seller often? Did you bargain today, and why or why not? Which language did you speak with the seller, and why that one? How did you pay? Did you see the sign about recording, and how do you feel about your purchase being recorded? Would you like a spoken confirmation of the price you agreed, for example from the seller's phone? Thank you.

### Appendix D. Elicitation script

Part one, prices. I will show you some amounts. Please say each one the way you would say it to a customer. The list runs fifty pesewas, one cedi, one cedi fifty, two cedis, five cedis, seven cedis fifty, ten cedis, twelve cedis, fifteen cedis, twenty cedis, twenty-five cedis, thirty cedis, forty-five cedis, fifty cedis, seventy-five cedis, one hundred cedis, one hundred and twenty cedis, two hundred and fifty cedis, five hundred cedis, one thousand cedis, two thousand five hundred cedis, ten thousand cedis. Now please say five of these the way an older customer might say them.

Part two, units. I will show you photographs. Please tell me what you call this measure and a price you might charge for it. The photographs show an olonka of maize, a bowl of gari, a heap of tomatoes, a heap of pepper, a bundle of onions, a crate of tomatoes, a jute sack of grain, a paint bucket, a tuber of yam, a bunch of plantain, a tilapia of each size grade you sell, a sachet of water, a tin of milk and a bag of rice.

Part three, speaking a sale. Imagine this phone keeps your records. For each situation, tell the phone what happened in your own words, as briefly or as fully as you like. A customer bought two heaps of tomatoes for ten cedis and paid cash. A customer bought one olonka of maize for twenty-five cedis and paid by mobile money. Your regular customer Ama took three tins of fish worth forty-five cedis and will pay on Friday. A customer bought onions for eight cedis and you added two extra onions. A customer bought tomatoes, pepper and onions and paid thirty cedis for everything. A customer owes you fifty cedis and paid twenty today. You want to know who owes you money. A customer returned a spoiled item and you gave back five cedis. You bought a crate of tomatoes from your supplier for two hundred cedis. You paid twenty cedis for transport this morning.

### Appendix E. Wizard-of-Oz script

Facilitator reads: This phone can keep your sales record. When you tell it a sale, it will repeat what it understood, and you say yes if it is right or correct it if it is wrong. Please tell it three sales you made today, one at a time.

After each utterance the facilitator, speaking as the system, reads back: You sold [quantity] [unit] of [item] for [total], paid by [mode]. Is that right? The facilitator records the trader's exact response, any correction, and any sign of confusion or irritation. After the third sale the facilitator asks, as the system: Would you like me to tell you your total for today? and notes the reaction. Finally the facilitator asks the trader, out of role, what felt natural and what felt strange.

### Appendix F. Stall signage text

Recording for research today. This seller is taking part in a University study of how people buy and sell. Sound is being recorded at this stall. If you do not want your voice recorded, tell the seller or the research assistant and your part will be deleted. No names or phone numbers are kept. Questions: [name and phone]. [Repeat in Twi, Ga or Ewe as appropriate, translated by a linguist.]

### Appendix G. Transcription convention, summary

Transcribe verbatim in the standard orthography of the language, including hesitations, repetitions and false starts, marking a false start with a trailing hyphen. Write numbers as words exactly as spoken and enter the numeric value in the separate amount field. Mark each token in a language other than the utterance's main language with a language suffix, for example ":eng" or ":twi", and do not tag proper names. Use the tags [noise], [laugh], [unclear], [disfluency] and [overlap]. Replace personal names with [name], phone numbers with [number] and mobile-money identifiers with [momo-id]. Do not correct grammar or dialect. A second person checks every transcript; the checker is never the transcriber.

## Part III. Consent instruments

All information sheets are read aloud in the participant's language by a facilitator fluent in that language, using a translation prepared by a qualified linguist and back-translated into English. Participants who cannot read may consent orally in the presence of a witness who is not part of the research team; the witness signs and the participant may add a thumbprint. Each participant receives a copy of the information sheet with the investigator's contact details. Text is written for reading aloud.

### G3. Customer verbal consent script

Spoken by the facilitator or the trader before or immediately after the customer's purchase, in the customer's language, with the response noted on the coding sheet.

Good morning. This stall is taking part in a University study of how people buy and sell, and sound is being recorded here today. Nobody's name or phone number is kept. Is it all right if your conversation with the seller is included? If you prefer not, just say so and we will delete it.

If the customer agrees, the facilitator marks "consented". If the customer declines, the facilitator marks "declined" with the time, and the audio for that transaction is deleted the same day. If the customer cannot be asked, the facilitator marks "not asked" and the transaction is excluded from transcription and release and kept only in the aggregate count. If the customer is a minor, the facilitator marks "minor" and the transaction is treated the same way.

### G4. Buyer exit interview verbal consent

May I ask you five short questions about your purchase? It takes two minutes, it is voluntary, you can stop at any time, and I will not ask your name. I will note your answers on this tablet and, if you agree, record your voice so I do not miss anything. You will receive [GH¢5] of airtime for your time. Is that all right, and may I record?

### G5. Market leader interview consent

Read as for G1 with the observation and microphone clauses removed and a single interview of about thirty minutes described. Statements 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7 of G1 apply, with the compensation of [GH¢40].

### G6. Optional video addendum, not used in phase one
