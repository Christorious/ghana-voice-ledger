# The Shape of the Sale: Trader-Customer Interaction, How Comparable Corpora Were Built, and What South Asia Is Doing

Companion to the data and feasibility memorandum in this folder. Three questions are answered in turn: what a trader-customer interaction in a Ghanaian market looks like and how it has been studied; exactly how the Senegalese, Malian, Burkinabè, Ghanaian, Nigerian, South African, Kenyan and Indian speech corpora cited in the memorandum were collected, so that their protocols can be copied; and whether South Asia is pursuing voice-based transaction capture and what can be borrowed from it.

## Evidence tags

**[V]** means a fetched page was read directly, which in this session meant GitHub repositories, including cloned copies of the WolBanking77, Kallaama, LaTeLL, Ashesi, University of Ghana, RobotsMali, IndicVoices, Common Voice, Harper Valley Bank, DSTC11 and SpokenWOZ repositories. **[S]** means the fact came from a search summary of a paper or page on a host this session could not open and should be checked before citation. **[E]** marks inference. Illustrative example phrases are marked as such and must not be treated as attested data.

## Part A. What a trader-customer interaction looks like

### A1. What has been studied, and what has not

Ghanaian market talk has been studied, but thinly and almost never with the transaction as the unit of analysis. The richest material is anthropological: Gracia Clark's four years of fieldwork in Kumasi Central Market, whose book has a chapter titled "Queens of Negotiation" and whose account of the 1979 price-control episode records that commodity queens brought samples of vegetables to regional offices so that size and quality grades could be fixed, an early documentation of the fact that Ghanaian foodstuff prices attach to graded heaps rather than to weight [S]; Claire Robertson's history of Ga women traders in Ussher Town [S]; and Kropp Dakubu's sociolinguistic history of language choice in Accra [S]. The one linguistic-anthropological line on bargaining talk itself is Sarah Monson's 2019 Indiana dissertation on women's communicative strategies in Kumasi Central Market and a 2022 Journal of Pragmatics paper based on bargaining interactions recorded there, which describes kasadɛ, "sweet talk", as a market speech genre combining Akan politeness with persuasion, in which traders use complimentary address forms and deference to pamper new and returning customers while also deploying rough, argumentative talk that blurs conflict and mock conflict [S]. A 2022 study of Agartha Market in Koforidua recorded persuasion and negotiation, found persuasion monologic and negotiation dialogic, and reported code-mixing of Twi and English as a common characteristic [S]. Agyekum's work on Akan greetings, leave-taking and the language of herbal drug sellers, and Afful's on non-kinship address terms, describe the openings and closings [S]. No Ghanaian study reports transaction-level statistics: turns, duration, the share of transactions that involve bargaining, the share of a sale that is verbalised, or the language mix by phase. Searches of the University of Ghana, KNUST, Cape Coast and Winneba repositories surfaced no linguistics thesis on market bargaining, which is a finding in itself, although those repositories index poorly and an on-site catalogue search is warranted [S]/[E]. Ga and Ewe market discourse are essentially undocumented beyond phrasebooks, and no linguistic description of credit talk, balance statements or mobile-money talk at the point of sale exists anywhere in West Africa in the sources found.

Nigeria has the densest literature on the structure of haggling. Ayoola secretly recorded haggling at Lagos meat stalls to identify its stages and strategies [S]; Alo and Soneye catalogued tactics in Ibadan and Lagos markets, greetings, humour, cajoling, flattery, pleading, swearing, abuse and code-switching [S]; a 2025 study across fifteen Igbo markets reports that a typical shopping routine takes at least an hour [S]; a 2022 study of buyer-seller common ground makes the observation that matters most for extraction, that a buyer is under no obligation to buy after haggling and a seller under none to sell [S]; and a conversation-analytic study of Hausa business talk finds that the buyer opens and may hold the floor while the seller contributes only minimal tokens [S]. These consistently describe a sequence of greeting and address, inquiry, quote, reduction with pleading or flattery, concession by price or by an added item, and payment and closing, carried in a mix of English, Pidgin and a local language. The classic literature adds Geertz on the bazaar as a response to information scarcity, Fafchamps on relational contracting and his finding with Minten that most agricultural transactions in Madagascar take place without orders and without credit, and Alexander on stable price-setting and trading partnerships in Javanese markets [S]. The service-encounter genre supplies the skeleton: Hasan and Ventola's obligatory stages of sale request, sale compliance, purchase and goods handover, Merritt's observation that a customer's request is often answered with a question rather than an answer, and an Ethiopian three-phase model from Bati market of inspection and inquiry, selection and bargaining, and deal, payment, thanks and goodbye [S].

### A2. A working model of the interaction

The model below is assembled from those sources. Phases 0, 2 and 6 are optional; phases 1, 3 and 5 are the minimal sale.

| Phase | Function | Proposed speech-act labels | Grounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Attract | Trader hails passers-by, praises goods | SUMMON, PRODUCT-PRAISE | Koforidua persuasion monologue; kasadɛ [S] |
| 1 Open | Greeting and kin or age address term set footing and often signal regular versus new customer | GREET, ADDRESS, SMALL-TALK | Agyekum on greetings; Afful; Monson [S] |
| 2 Inspect | Buyer names or points at item, asks quality, size or unit; seller asks what is wanted | ITEM-REQUEST, ITEM-CLARIFY, QUALITY-ASSERT, UNIT-SPECIFY | Merritt; Bati beginning phase [S] |
| 3 Price | Price ask and seller's quote, usually bound to a unit | PRICE-ASK, NEW-OFFER, UNIT-BIND, PRICE-JUSTIFY | Bargaining acts of Heddaya et al.; Twi frames [S] |
| 4 Negotiate | Reduction request, pleading, flattery, mock conflict, counter-offers, "last price", concession by price or by added quantity, walk-away | REDUCE-REQUEST, COUNTER-OFFER, PUSH, COMPARISON, ALLOWANCE, DASH-OFFER, LAST-PRICE, REFUSE, WALK-AWAY | Ayoola; Alo and Soneye; the Ghanaian "dash" [S] |
| 5 Agree | Buyer accepts explicitly, by a quantity imperative, or silently by producing money; quantity fixed; baskets loop back to phase 2 | ACCEPT, QUANTITY-SPECIFY, ADD-ITEM, TOTAL-STATE | No obligation to buy after haggling; Hausa minimal seller tokens [S] |
| 6 Settle | Cash and change, mobile money, or credit with balance talk | PAY-CASH, CHANGE, PAY-MOMO, CREDIT-REQUEST, CREDIT-GRANT, BALANCE-STATE | "momo" as a verb; about 27 percent of informal firms extend trade credit [S] |
| 7 Close | Bagging, thanks, blessing, leave-taking | HANDOVER, THANK, BLESS, LEAVE-TAKE | Hasan's goods handover; Agyekum on leave-taking [S] |

Several structural properties follow. Phases 3 and 4 may iterate several times. Phase 4 is often skipped for regular customers and for staples whose prices are set by section-level convention, which a 2023 study of "the myth of the market queens" suggests are anchored by convention rather than by queens' power [S]. The seller may say almost nothing. The buyer may switch language mid-encounter. Closing is frequently multimodal, with little speech.

### A3. Numerals, units and money in speech

Prices are spoken in at least four numeral systems. Twi counts baako, mmienu, mmiɛnsa, ɛnan, enum, nsia, nson, nwɔtwe, nkron, du, then aduonu for twenty, aduasa, aduanan, aduonum for fifty, ɔha for a hundred and apem for a thousand, with price frames such as "Wei yɛ sɛn?" answered by "Ɛyɛ aduonum cedis" and debt frames such as "Ɔde Yaa ka cedi num" for owing five cedis [S]. Ga counts ekome, enyɔ, etɛ, ejwɛ, enumɔ, ekpaa, kpawo, kpaanyɔ, nɛɛhu, nyɔŋma, with oha for a hundred and akpe for a thousand and shika for money [S]. Ewe counts ɖeka, eve, etɔ̃, ene, atɔ̃, adé, adrẽ, enyí, asíéke, ewó, with blaeve for twenty, literally two bundles of ten, alafa ɖeka for a hundred and akpe ɖeka for a thousand, and asks "Nenie nyé éyà?" for how much [S]. English numerals are embedded pervasively in Twi speech, which is why the KasaSpeech corpus exists [S]. Money is counted in cedis and pesewas, "Ghana" serves as a colloquial unit for the new cedi in guides though no linguistic source documents it [E], and old-cedi thousands persist so that a spoken "twenty" may mean twenty thousand old cedis, that is two new cedis, or twenty new cedis [S]. A price parser must therefore resolve twenty, aduonu, twenty thousand and two Ghana against commodity-specific priors.

Quantities are stated in non-standard units whose sizes vary by region: olonka and other repurposed tins, bowls, heaps, bundles, sacks, basins, buckets, crates, tubers by the hundred, and fish by "size". On 7 July 2026 the Ghana Statistical Service released the country's first national conversion factors for these units from a survey of markets, households and farm gates, finding for instance that an olonka of dried white maize is 2.55 kilograms nationally while a jute sack of yellow maize ranges from 131 kilograms in Greater Accra to 198 in Bono East, and it maintains a web conversion system [S]. Sellers often concede with an extra item, the dash, rather than a lower price, so a concession can change quantity while the price holds [S]. Guides put opening quotes 30 to 50 percent above the accepted price, a figure with no scholarly support [S].

### A4. What defines a sale event

A sale event, for extraction, is a closed sub-sequence in which an item referent is established, a price commitment exists that is not displaced by a later quote, the buyer accepts explicitly, by a quantity imperative after a quote, or behaviourally by tendering money or initiating mobile money while the seller bags without objection, a quantity and unit are determinable or default to the customary unit for that commodity, and settlement or deferral occurs by cash, mobile money or acknowledged credit [E]. A quote alone establishes nothing, since neither party is bound by a haggle [S], and the "Language of Bargaining" annotation scheme keeps new and repeated offers distinct from the end act of acceptance [S]. The last numeric mention before a payment or handover act is the transaction price; a "last price" utterance is a final offer, not an agreement, unless followed by acceptance. Multi-item baskets may have a single spoken total, so the record must accept a basket total with item lines lacking prices. A dash changes quantity while the price holds. Credit produces an unpaid line with a counterparty whose balance may be stated in old-cedi thousands or as "the remaining" with no number. No source quantifies how much of a market sale is verbalised; the Hausa study's minimal seller tokens and the Malagasy cash-and-carry finding suggest that silent closings are common [S]. The design consequence is that passive detection will miss silent closings and that the canonical record must be a trader-initiated utterance, such as "two heaps tomato, ten Ghana, cash", which is how the Nigerian prototypes work [V].

### A5. A field study to build the interaction picture

The aim is a consented, coded corpus of real transactions across languages and commodity types, with transaction-level statistics, a validated annotation scheme, and elicited numeral, unit and price inventories for Twi, Ga, Ewe and Pidgin. Three or four sites give language coverage: Makola or Kaneshie for the Ga, Twi, English and Pidgin mix; Kejetia for Asante Twi and comparability with Monson and Clark; Tema Community 1 or Ho for Ewe and Ga; and Tamale or Techiman as a fourth if budget allows. Four commodity strata separate the unit systems: vegetables by heap, grains and flours by olonka or bowl, fish by size or piece, and provisions by piece as a fixed-price control. Eight to twelve consenting traders per site across those strata, each observed for two half-day sessions covering the morning peak and the afternoon, yield 30 to 60 transactions per trader, or roughly 1,500 to 2,500 transactions in all, of which about 400 would be transcribed and fully annotated. Comparable linguistic studies used far less speech, ten recordings in one Nigerian study and nine excerpts in another, while attitude surveys were large, 945 traders at Makola for mobile money [S].

The methods are complementary. A structured observation sheet records one row per transaction: time, customer type, languages by phase, address term, price-ask form, quote form with numeral system and unit, reduction requests, number of offers, whether a dash was requested or given, acceptance type, quantity and unit, item count, payment mode, change talk, closing form, duration, turns and phases with no speech. Audio is captured by a lapel microphone on the trader and a boundary microphone, with video of the counter only where market authorities and traders agree, to capture gesture and handover. Short exit interviews with a subsample of buyers and semi-structured interviews with traders cover credit practice, mobile money, how prices are said, "last price" norms, and how they would want to speak a sale to a device. Elicitation tasks per language collect a spoken price list in new cedis, old cedis and each language's numerals, unit names from photographs, and "log a sale" utterances for scripted cash, mobile-money, credit, dash and basket scenarios. A Wizard-of-Oz test of the bookkeeper prompt measures the natural form of trader-initiated records, mirroring the Ashesi design. Annotation segments transactions, labels phases, tags utterances with ISO 24617-2 general-purpose functions plus a domain layer merging the bargaining acts with sale slots for item, quantity, unit, unit price, total, payment mode, counterparty and credit balance, and the Ghana-specific acts for dash, last price, credit request and mobile-money payment. Twenty percent is double-annotated for agreement, and the verbalisation ratio, slots recoverable from speech alone divided by slots recoverable from speech plus observation, is computed as the study's headline number. Ethics require institutional approval, permission from market management and commodity queens, consent from traders in their language, visible bilingual signage at each stall with buyer opt-out honoured by deletion, no covert recording, pseudonymisation, removal of names and mobile-money numbers, compensation for traders' time, and return of findings to the market association; the Ivorian experience of vendors' reluctance argues for recruiting through queens and association leaders rather than by cold approach [S]/[E].

## Part B. How the comparable corpora were built

### B1. Corpus by corpus

The Senegalese WolBanking77 corpus has two layers. Its text layer is 9,791 banking-domain sentences across 77 intents, by name a translation of the English Banking77 set. Its speech layer is a read-speech lexicon of 186 customer queries and 77 system responses stored as CSV rows pairing a French source sentence, its intent, the Wolof translation and a bracketed phonetic transcription, for example "consulter le solde de mon compte" rendered as "Seet sama tolluwaayu kont." with its pronunciation, so that different readers pronounce consistently [V]. The intent inventory is a scripted dialogue-system prompt and response set, with balance queries the largest class, and the audio protocol follows the MINDS-14 model of native speakers recording each intent as a crowd task [S]. Speaker counts, recording device, consent and payment are not documented in the repository; the corpus is over four hours, CC-BY-4.0, hosted on Kaggle, and its very low word error rates after fine-tuning are what clean read speech of a closed prompt set produces [V].

Kallaama, also Senegalese, is found speech rather than elicited: radio shows, interviews, focus groups, push messages and voice messages about agriculture whose audio belongs to the company Jokalante, 125 hours in Wolof, Pulaar and Sereer, each recording rated one to five for processing difficulty [V]. Only 11 to 20 percent of the speech is by women. Transcription used the Transcriber tool with two layers, raw transcripts by three linguistics students on summer internships and a checked layer validated by a supervisor other than the transcriber, but only 35 of the 125 hours received the second pass within the one-year Lacuna grant [V]. Its conventions tag disfluencies and mark loanwords with a language suffix such as "word :fra", and the README documents that tag spellings drifted across transcribers [V]. The lesson is to lint tags automatically and to budget transcription before recording.

Kunkado, from RobotsMali in Mali, was compiled from about 300 hours of radio and television archives donated by four broadcasters under written arrangement, segmented at silences into 118,925 pieces totalling 161 hours, pre-transcribed by the group's own Bambara model and then manually corrected for about 40 hours, a quarter of the total, in the project's timeframe [V]. Numbers were transcribed as digits for speed, code-switches marked with double underscores, and a French tag set marks noise, overlap, laughter, music and other events; the card lists known issues including inconsistent digit use [V]. Fine-tuning on the 33 reviewed hours cut word error from 44 to 37 percent [S]. RobotsMali's companion Bambara collection under the African Next Voices programme is the more transferable protocol: 626 raw hours from 512 speakers gathered by trained facilitators who guided participants into natural topic-focused conversation, using a Flutter app that records mono 44.1 kHz audio, computes signal-to-noise ratio and silence share on the spot, and stores a facilitator profile and a participant sociolinguistic profile covering mother tongue, other languages with proficiency, education and birthplace [V]. The app code contains no consent screen, and the consent process is not documented [V].

The Luxembourg Mooré-French ledger corpus is the closest in task. Its three layers are a synthetic pool of 4,570 one-clause merchant statements generated from templates and voiced by text-to-speech in male and female voices, in four conditions from pure Mooré to code-switched with the amount and name in French, such as "A Seku tara maam cinquante-sept mille." or the Mooré "biiga tara maam wakɩr tus piig la a yoobe la kobs-naase." meaning the child owes me 82,000, with amounts following the wakir system in which one wakir is five CFA francs [V]; a real development set of 20 source intents recorded by six speakers, 196 files, from French prompts such as "Moussa me doit 2000.", "La vendeuse de beignets me doit 45000.", "Fatou a pris marchandise 250000, elle va payer après." and "Qui me doit de l'argent ?" which the speakers rendered into Mooré or code-switched speech themselves [V]/[E]; and a sealed holdout from a separate cohort of seven speakers, 60 intents, 312 files, recorded over a few weeks in mid-2026 and scored exactly once after the system was frozen [V]. Ground truth follows a JSON schema with intent group, currency, lists of debts up and down as entity and amount pairs, and typed queries; the real ground truth was drafted by a language model from the clean French source and then hand-verified with zero corrections, and the authors insist that ground truth derives from clean text and never from recogniser output [V]. Entities are scored by phonetic skeleton because users name customers by voice. The repository states as a limitation that no held-out real test set existed at submission time, which is why the fresh cohort was recorded, and it frames the product requirement in one sentence: the primary user may not be able to proofread written entries, so prefer valid JSON, confidence tiers, abstention and a spoken confirmation loop [V]. Demographics, recruitment, device, consent and payment are not documented, and there is no licence file.

The Ashesi and Nokwary Financial Inclusion corpus is read speech: about 130 sentences per speaker, the same for all speakers of a variety, drawn from prompts of a Wizard-of-Oz financial services application, sentences supplied by linguists for phonetic balance, and common phrases, recorded by about 200 speakers per variety in Akuapem Twi, Asante Twi, Fante and Ga through what the compressed .ogg format suggests was a phone application [V]/[E]. The filename encodes language, sex and age. Quality was audited by a stratified five percent sample, with mismatch rates from 0.1 percent for Ga to 2.8 percent for Akuapem Twi, and the authors state plainly that their ten percent archive is not a strict test set because speakers and phrases overlap with the ninety percent [V]. Device, consent and payment are not documented.

The University of Ghana corpus and Google's WAXAL use image description: speakers describe culturally relevant images in a custom Android application, producing clips of 15 to 30 seconds whose metadata record device, environment, year, sex, age and locale [S]/[V]. The Ewe split shows what the field looks like: 163 training speakers aged 18 to 86, environments split almost evenly between indoors and outdoors, and devices dominated by a lab-issued Samsung tablet alongside volunteers' own Tecno, Infinix and itel phones [V]. The split is speaker-disjoint at 70, 20, 8 and 2 percent with a secret set [V]. About ten percent is transcribed, and the lab provides keyboards for thirteen African languages to its transcribers [V]. The lab's newer spontaneous branch uses themed question banks, for instance eighteen sheets of about a hundred maternal-health questions each, one of which asks whether it is safe to continue selling at the market while pregnant [V]. Consent and payment are not documented.

NaijaVoices had 144 language users and experts write some 645,000 culturally rich sentences from 100 themes under double review, then had facilitators with technical and linguistic training guide more than 5,000 voice donors through recording in an application, 240 sentences per donor in the first phase, with a small second phase of about a hundred donors reading 6,000 sentences each for text-to-speech [S]. Swivuriso in South Africa recruited through digital campaigns, community outreach and universities, screened candidates by a voice-based proficiency test, required identity and facial verification against fraud, had participants sign consent contracts, capped sessions at twenty minutes for vocal fatigue with multiple sessions to about an hour per speaker, had language teams review recordings before trained annotators transcribed in the same platform, and linked utterances to anonymised metadata by identifier with personal information encrypted separately [S]. AfriVoices-KE in Kenya combined 750 scripted hours from eleven domains with 2,250 spontaneous hours elicited by text and image prompts from 4,777 speakers in a custom smartphone app that validated signal-to-noise ratio before accepting a take [S]. IndicVoices in India is 8 percent read, 76 percent extempore and 15 percent conversational, gathered through a Flutter app built on Microsoft's Karya toolkit whose task types include recording a sentence, describing an image and refining audio, with coordinators who explain purpose, time and compensation, take consent on a form and register participants; its release ships parallel verbatim and normalised transcripts and a controlled inventory of bracketed noise tags, and transcription ran on the open Shoonya platform [V]/[S]. Project Vaani has speakers describe up to fifty images because images have fewer structural constraints than text, are accessible to non-literate speakers and reduce social-desirability bias, with more than a thousand speakers per district [S].

Common Voice remains the community baseline and its rules are the most fully published: sentences of at most fourteen words, speakable, without digits because "2409" is ambiguous, two up-votes to validate a sentence or a clip, accents welcome and clips rejected only for misreadings, another audible speaker or extreme volume; and for its new spontaneous and code-switching modalities, prompts answerable in a couple of sentences within fifteen to thirty seconds, verbatim transcription including hesitations, numbers spelled out in words, false starts marked with a hyphen, tags for laughter, disfluency, unclear speech and noise, and a rule that the checker is never the transcriber [V]. Three spoken-dialogue protocols complete the picture. DSTC11's speech-aware MultiWOZ recorded only user turns, in three conditions, synthesised, human-verbatim and human-paraphrased, to test whether synthesis is a reasonable surrogate for human recording [V]. SpokenWOZ paired a user reading a template-generated goal with a human agent over the telephone, admitted only 250 of 1,520 applicants after a qualification test on task completion and naturalness, and removed unnatural or failed dialogues by crowd rating [S]. Harper Valley Bank generated eight banking task templates with randomised slot values such as a money amount, paired callers and agents by telephony, gave the caller a persona, had the caller mark task completion and both parties rate the call, and had transcriptionists label script adherence and audio quality; the 1,446 calls from 59 speakers form the only permissively licensed spoken corpus of financial amounts in dialogue [V]. RobotsMali's mobileBAMking set of 588 annotated Bambara banking voice commands and its narrow QuartzNum recogniser for spoken amounts, account numbers and phone numbers are the closest African analogues [V].

### B2. A protocol for a Ghanaian spoken-transaction corpus

The design principle is one pipeline with one metadata schema, one ground-truth schema and one transcription convention across three tiers, so that scripted data trains the amount and entity recogniser, role play trains dialogue structure, and consented real sales form the honest test bed. The common backbone borrows the Luxembourg ground-truth schema extended with sale lines of item, quantity, unit price and total, a payment method of cash, mobile money or credit, and amounts as integer pesewas, with ground truth always derived from clean text rather than recogniser output [V]. Transcription keeps a verbatim tier and a normalised tier as IndicVoices does, writes amounts as spoken in the verbatim tier with the numeric value in the ground-truth field rather than following Kunkado's digits shortcut, marks code-switches with a machine-checkable per-token language suffix in Kallaama's style with an automatic lint, and uses the Common Voice tag inventory plus an overlap tag and the false-start hyphen [V]. Metadata per clip combines the University of Ghana fields for speaker, locale, sex, age, device, environment and year with the RobotsMali sociolinguistic profile and in-app signal-to-noise and silence measures, and separates personal information under an identifier as Swivuriso does [V]/[S]. Splits are speaker-disjoint with a secret set, and a fresh-cohort sealed test set is recorded after the system is frozen and scored once [V]. Quality control applies a two-person rule everywhere, a five percent stratified audit with a per-language bad-clip rate, and a signal-to-noise gate before a take is accepted. Consent is written or recorded, explains purpose, time and compensation before registration, instructs participants to avoid personal content, and provides a report flag; since none of the twelve corpora publishes its consent text, the project should write its own and publish it. The licence is CC-BY-4.0. Budget anchors are 30 to 36 labour-hours per audio hour for careful transcription of an oral language and the observation that two well-funded projects checked only a quarter to a third of their audio within a grant cycle [S]/[V].

The scripted tier borrows WolBanking77's intent inventory with source, translation and phonetic guide, translated by a university linguistics unit as the Dakar centre did; Ashesi's mix of application prompts, phonetically balanced sentences and common phrases at about 130 sentences per speaker and 200 speakers per variety with a five percent audit; the Luxembourg template generator over entities, amounts and transaction shapes with splits stratified by amount bucket so that prices from fifty pesewas to ten thousand cedis are covered; Common Voice's sentence rules except the ban on numbers, writing amounts in words in the prompt and storing the value separately; a Karya-style microtask app with per-sentence re-recording; and DSTC11's paraphrase condition for ten to twenty percent of prompts to escape pure reading register [V]. The role-play tier borrows Harper Valley Bank's random task templates and personas, translated into a seller card with items, prices, stock and credit policy and a buyer card with goal items, quantities, a bargaining target, payment method and partial credit, whose slot values become the gold ledger; SpokenWOZ's qualification test on completion and naturalness; IndicVoices' conversational scenarios and coordinators; Swivuriso's session cap, screening and consent contract; Common Voice's code-switching prompts about high-stakes moments and exact words to elicit natural mobile-money talk; two-channel recording; and the seller's spoken read-back of the ledger as an extra utterance type [V]/[S]. The real-sales tier borrows the RobotsMali facilitator app with its offline recording and on-device quality measures, Kallaama's complexity rating and raw-versus-checked layers, Kunkado's acceptance of messy audio with overlap and noise tags, voice-activity segmentation followed by model pre-labels and human correction, Common Voice's verbatim rules, and Swivuriso's separation of personal data; consent pairs a signed seller contract with a stall signboard and a recorded verbal consent from each customer, with deletion on request and names replaced by a placeholder in released transcripts while the phonetic-skeleton entity label is kept in ground truth [V]/[E]. This tier serves first as sealed test and development data and grows into training data as transcription budget allows.

## Part C. What South Asia is doing

### C1. The direct answer

South Asia is pursuing voice for money strongly at the infrastructure and payments layers and only thinly and recently at the bookkeeping layer, so the intuition is half right. Voice payment is state policy in India: the National Payments Corporation launched Hello UPI conversational voice payments in 2023 with the Bhashini programme and AI4Bharat at IIT Madras, UPI 123Pay gives feature-phone users payments over an interactive voice line, and a conversational assistant was added to the BHIM app in September 2026 in five languages [S]. Spoken confirmation of received payments is a mass-market success: Paytm reported 13.7 million paying merchant device subscriptions in the second quarter of its 2026 financial year, its Soundbox announces receipts in eleven languages, and Bangladesh's TallyKhata added voice payment notifications [S]. India's speech-data programmes are the largest paid, district-sampled efforts anywhere, with IndicVoices at some 23,700 hours from 51,000 speakers across more than 400 districts and Project Vaani at 31,278 hours from 156,000 speakers [S]. Voice entry of ledger records, by contrast, is a startup and hackathon layer. The one commercial product found is VoiceKhata, which sells push-to-talk entry in twelve languages for 199 rupees a month; Khatabook, OkCredit and Vyapar show no voice entry; Bangladesh's Hishab launched a voice-call ledger with the operator Robi in 2018 for entrepreneurs without literacy or internet and has since repositioned as a telephony generative-AI company; and a burst of 2025 and 2026 prototypes across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh exists on GitHub without a single field evaluation [S]/[V]. No published usage, retention or evaluation figures were found for Hello UPI, 123Pay or any voice khata product. The best design evidence is a Level One Project note that low 123Pay adoption was driven by the friction of retrieving two one-time passwords during onboarding, while users embraced voice and gained confidence when names and amounts were repeated before a transfer [S]. One correction to an earlier report in this folder: the Smart India Hackathon problem statement numbered 26091 is titled an AI-driven business advisory and financial structuring assistant for rural micro-entrepreneurs, not a voice ledger, although several teams added voice ledgers on their own [V].

### C2. Methods worth copying

The Indian programmes offer methods rather than a proven bookkeeping product. IndicVoices releases a blueprint of standardised protocols, engaging question repositories and conversation scenarios, quality-control layers and transcription guidelines, paid participants at the prevailing district daily wage under ethics approval with instructions in the native language, and reached a mix of three quarters extempore, a sixth conversational and a twelfth read; its Kathbath front end sits on Karya and its Shoonya annotation platform is open and self-hostable [S]/[V]. Project Vaani's image prompts remove literacy barriers and reduce social-desirability bias; photographs of stalls, produce, scales and mobile-money kiosks would be the Ghanaian equivalents [S]. Karya pays a minimum of about five dollars per hour of work, roughly twenty times the Indian minimum wage, takes a platform share that has fallen toward a fifth, distributes all resale revenue to the contributing workers in proportion to hours, onboards workers over WhatsApp, and already operates in Kenya and Ethiopia with pilots in South Africa and Bangladesh; its open recorder component is reusable [S]/[V]. Bengali.AI built its training set through Common Voice campaigns at 400 hours in two months and its test set from seventeen real-world sources such as television drama and sermons, which is the pattern for a Ghanaian test set drawn from real market audio rather than from the crowd that produced the training data [S]. The SRUTI project recruited a hundred rural Bhojpuri-speaking women specifically and used synthesis to cut error, and a national Indian benchmark found a systematic penalty of about a fifth for male speakers and catastrophic failure for out-of-region migrants, so sampling by sex, age and migration status matters [S]. Digital Green's released pipeline includes a rule-based grapheme-to-phoneme module with phonetic edit distance, a number and spelling normaliser, precision-first correction of domain terms from a weighted lexicon, a criticality-weighted word error rate in which pesticide names count four times and crops three times, and a weight-free speaker selector that transfers across languages; a ledger word error rate in which amounts and counterparty names carry four times the weight would be its direct descendant [V]. The indic-punct project implements inverse text normalisation for twelve Indic languages as weighted finite-state grammars that turn spoken lakh-and-crore numbers into digits and handle currency, and Akan, Ga and Ewe numeral and cedi grammars should be written the same way [V]. Josh Talks recorded 26,000 hours of two-person Hindi conversation remotely on assigned topics, a cheap way to elicit haggling and credit talk by pairing a seller and a buyer at a distance [S]. Gooey's evaluation service lets an organisation upload its own gold-transcribed audio and score competing recognisers against it, which is the discipline of keeping a 200-utterance Twi, Ga and Ewe gold set and re-running every candidate model [S]. No public spoken corpus of shop or bazaar transactions exists in Hindi, Bangla, Tamil or Urdu [E].

### C3. What to borrow and what to avoid

Five things transfer directly. Read the entry back before committing it, in the user's language, and give a spoken receipt afterward: 123Pay users' confidence rose when names and amounts were repeated, the Bangladeshi EKASH design added mandatory reconfirmation for low-literacy users, a Pakistani prototype synthesises a confirmation before the shopkeeper approves, and the soundbox experience shows that an audible announcement is itself a trust device [S]/[V]. Keep the language model for extraction only and put numbers, validation and dialogue flow in deterministic code: FormBharo keeps validation, retries and branching rule-based and still reports a drop of about 41 points in end-to-end completion when its models face real input [S]. Run paid, sampled, prompt-driven data work with an out-of-distribution test set on self-hosted open tooling. Distribute through WhatsApp voice notes first and a plain phone call second, which is how Awaaz-e-Sehat moved from a clinician app to WhatsApp and how Hishab and Gram Vaani reach people without smartphones [S]. Evaluate on task success rather than word error, as SpeakPay's transaction success rate and Digital Green's weighted error do [S]/[V].

Three things should not be borrowed. The free-app-monetise-later model failed: Khatabook and OkCredit shut their storefront pivots and cut staff, Vyapar lost 63 crore rupees in its 2025 financial year with cash reserves down 93 percent, while what monetised was a paid device or subscription tied to payments, and VoiceKhata charges from the first day [S]. Cloud-only prototypes built on frontier models with no offline path and no evaluation, which describes every 2025 and 2026 voice-ledger repository inspected, should not be the template [V]. And the assumption that general multilingual recognition works for a low-resource language should be dropped: zero-shot Whisper produced 130 percent word error on Nepali financial commands, and the national Indian benchmark shows four to five times higher error for Bhojpuri and Maithili than for Hindi [S].

## What this adds to the memorandum

Three conclusions feed back into the plan. First, the field study in Part A should precede any large recording effort, because nobody has measured how much of a Ghanaian sale is spoken, and that verbalisation ratio decides whether ambient capture can ever work. Second, the corpus protocol in Part B should reuse the Luxembourg ground-truth schema, the University of Ghana metadata, the Common Voice transcription rules and the Harper Valley Bank role-play templates, and should publish its consent text because none of its predecessors did. Third, South Asia supplies methods and design findings, above all read-back before commit and deterministic handling of numbers, and it supplies a warning about business models, but it does not yet supply a proven voice bookkeeper to copy.
