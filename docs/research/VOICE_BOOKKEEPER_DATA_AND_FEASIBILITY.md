# A Voice Bookkeeper for Ghanaian Traders: Data Landscape and Feasibility

Research memorandum, 30 September 2026. Prepared for the ghana-voice-ledger and sikabook-models repositories.

## How to read the evidence in this document

Every factual claim below carries one of three tags. **[V]** means the source file was read directly during this study: a licence file, a benchmark result file, a dataset README, or code in the two repositories. **[S]** means the figure came from a search-engine summary of the named source, because the session's network policy denied direct access to almost every host outside github.com (Hugging Face, arXiv, Zenodo, Kaggle, Mozilla, vendor documentation, Ghanaian government and university sites were all blocked). **[E]** marks an estimate or a design inference. Anything tagged [S] should be re-read at the source before it is quoted in a grant application or a paper. The list of items that most need re-verification is at the end.

**Purpose of the project.** The author has stated that the project is non-commercial and in the public interest: the tool is to be free to traders, its aim is to widen their access to the financial resources available in Ghana and to give them direct insight into their own day-to-day trading, and its data will be released openly. Section 4.1 sets out what that changes for licensing, and it should be repeated in ethics applications, funding applications and the app's consent screen.

## 1. Summary

The project is feasible in a narrower form than the one described in the repositories, and the narrowing is dictated by data. Measured on an independent Ghanaian benchmark, the best speech recogniser available for Twi transcribes free speech with a word error rate near 45 percent, and every general-purpose cloud model, including Gemini and every Whisper variant, performs far worse [V]. That floor rules out the always-listening agent that transcribes bargaining at a fish stall and silently books the sale. It does not rule out a voice bookkeeper. On the finance-domain read speech that already exists for Twi, Fante and Ga, a 153-million-parameter open model reaches a word error rate near 19 percent [V], which is workable when the recognition task is constrained to item, quantity and price, the amount is read back before it is booked, and the user speaks into the phone rather than across a stall.

Three findings from the code should shape the plan before any data work starts. The Android app has no working speech recognition: the offline recogniser returns canned strings chosen by audio buffer length, the voice activity detector is simulated, speaker identification falls back to a mock embedding, and the Google Cloud path is disabled [V]. The status report's claims of 98 percent completion and 95 percent recognition accuracy therefore describe the user interface and database shell, not a voice system. The models repository holds a sound plan and a working rules-based extractor, but its licence matrix contains errors that matter: FLEURS has no Akan configuration [V], Meta's newer Omnilingual ASR is Apache-2.0 rather than licence-blocked like MMS [V], and the Twi-English code-switching corpus carries an Apache-2.0 tag on Hugging Face rather than the assumed non-commercial default [S]. Finally, the Kiro prompt documents in the app repository gloss two Akan numerals wrongly, reading "dunum" as ten and "aduonum" as twenty when they mean fifteen and fifty; the Python rules in sikabook-models have the correct values [V].

The data situation is better than a first search suggests and worse than the repositories assume. Around 150 hours of permissively licensed Akan and Ga speech exist in the finance domain, roughly 250 hours of Ghanaian-language speech under CC-BY-4.0 arrived in 2026 through Google's WAXAL release, and 95 hours of Twi-English code-switched speech may be usable after a licence confirmation. Nothing conversational, nothing recorded in a market, and nothing in Ghanaian Pidgin exists anywhere. The 5,000-hour University of Ghana corpus, the largest in the country, is licensed for non-commercial use with no derivatives, so it cannot train a model for commercial distribution, although a non-commercial project such as this one may use it under the conditions in section 4.1 [V]. The gap between what exists and what the product needs is therefore a specific, affordable one: on the order of 20 to 50 hours of consented, in-domain, structured sale utterances, which a small team can collect for a few thousand dollars in one season.

The project also sits in an identifiable research field. A Luxembourg group has published the only quantified voice-to-ledger pipeline for an African language, reaching 76 to 85 percent amount accuracy on Mooré and Mooré-French speech, Microsoft Research Africa has published the only field study of voice entry at a shop counter, and a large wave of unevaluated prototypes exists on every continent. No voice bookkeeper for informal traders has published user evidence in any language, so a measured Ghanaian deployment would be a first (section 10).

## 2. Where the two repositories actually stand

The ghana-voice-ledger app is a Kotlin, Jetpack Compose, Room and Hilt codebase with a genuine transaction store, offline queue, encryption, summaries, onboarding and a substantial test suite. Its speech layer is scaffolding. In `OfflineSpeechRecognizer.kt` the model load is a logged no-op and `simulateOfflineTranscription` maps buffers under 5 kB to "how much", under 10 kB to "how much is this fish" and everything longer to "how much is this tilapia per piece" [V]. `WebRTCVADProcessor.kt` documents its native calls as simulated [V]. `TensorFlowLiteSpeakerIdentifier.kt` generates a mock embedding when the model file is missing, and the bundled `speaker_embedding_model.tflite` has no provenance in the repository [V]. The Google Cloud recogniser is renamed to `.disabled` and its dependency is commented out in the Gradle file [V]. The entity extractor in `GhanaEntityExtractor.kt` handles English numerals and cedi and pesewa patterns; it has no Twi or Ga numerals [V].

The sikabook-models repository is the more honest of the two. Its README dates the status to 2 September 2026 and describes a Vosk English baseline that cannot recognise "cedis" natively because the compiled lexicon is closed, a rules extractor with 45 passing tests, a synthetic NLU set, a fine-tuning recipe for a streaming zipformer that has not yet been run, and an optional small language model tier gated on device latency [V]. Its device analysis is right: the target is a 4 GB Helio G81 or Unisoc phone with CPU-only inference. Its `numbers.py` maps Twi numerals correctly, including "aduonu" to 20 and "aduonum" to 50 [V].

## 3. The measured state of Ghanaian-language speech recognition

The central empirical input for this study is GhanaNLP's open ASR benchmark, whose per-model result files were read directly from GitHub [V]. It scores organisation-owned models on a 43-variety evaluation set with up to 1,000 clips per domain and reports the mean over domains. The domains present for each language differ, and this matters for interpretation: Asante Twi is scored on bible, finance, lds, unicef and waxal material, Akuapem Twi on bible and finance, Ga on finance and jw, Fante on bible, finance, jw and lds, and Ewe on bible, jw, unicef and waxal.

| Akuapem Twi | griot-nano-1 | 0.433 | 0.196 | 0.558 | 0.884 | 0.653 |
| Ga | griot-nano-1 | 0.412 | 0.030 | not run | 1.031 | 1.212 |

Source: benchmarks/{twi_asante,twi_akuapem,gaa,fat,ewe}.yaml in github.com/GhanaNLP/nsanku-asr-benchmark, runs dated August and September 2026 [V].

Three observations follow. First, the "finance" domain is almost certainly the Ashesi and Nokwary Financial Inclusion Speech Dataset, since its language coverage is exactly Akuapem Twi, Asante Twi, Fante and Ga [E]. The near-zero Ga finance error of griot-nano-1 indicates that this model was trained on that corpus, so its finance numbers measure in-domain read speech rather than generalisation. Second, the same model's error on Twi material from other domains sits between 31 and 70 percent, which is the honest estimate of what a model trained on today's public data does on speech it has not seen. Third, the gap between Meta's self-reported character error rate for Akan (2.5 percent on its own test data, trained on 45 hours [V]) and its 72 percent word error rate on this benchmark [V] is the clearest available demonstration of domain shift in Ghanaian ASR. Any plan that assumes a downloaded model will work in a market without in-domain data is contradicted by these files.

Whisper has no Akan, Ewe, Ga or Dagbani language token at all [V], so every "Whisper Twi" fine-tune is learning the language from scratch inside a decoder built for other languages; on the benchmark such fine-tunes score between 84 and 136 percent word error rate [V]. Speech-capable large language models fail similarly, and GhanaNLP notes that Gemini 2.5 Flash returned an English sentence when given Twi audio [V].

For on-device use, one development changes the picture from what sikabook-models assumed. Meta's Omnilingual ASR, released in November 2025, is licensed Apache-2.0 for both code and weights [V], covers `aka_Latn`, `twi_Latn`, `fat_Latn`, `ewe_Latn`, `dag_Latn`, `hau_Latn` and `pcm_Latn` but not Ga [V], and its 300-million-parameter CTC variant is already exported by sherpa-onnx as a 348 MB int8 ONNX model with a working Android sample application that pairs it with Silero voice activity detection [V]. Its zero-shot accuracy on Twi is poor (word error rate 0.82 to 0.87 on the benchmark [V]), which is why it is a fine-tuning base rather than a product, but it is the only permissively licensed multilingual model with Akan in its pretraining that can be fine-tuned and shipped. MMS remains CC-BY-NC-4.0 [V].

Coverage by language is uneven in ways that should drive sequencing. Akan is the only language with more than a hundred permissively licensed hours. Ga exists almost solely through the Ashesi corpus, and Omnilingual ASR does not list it, so a Ga recogniser must be trained rather than adapted. Two of these findings correct the sikabook-models licence matrix: FLEURS contains Hausa and no Akan [V], and WAXAL covers 21 languages of which six are Ghanaian, not 27 [S].


### 4.1 What changes because the project is non-commercial

The author has stated that the project has no commercial purpose: the tool is to be free to traders, is meant to widen their access to finance and their insight into their own businesses, and will release its data openly. This changes the licence analysis in one direction only. Creative Commons non-commercial clauses restrict the use rather than the user, so a free tool with no revenue, grant-funded research and published papers fall within them, and several resources marked unusable in the table become available: the University of Ghana corpus with roughly 518 transcribed hours across Akan, Ewe, Dagbani and Dagaare, GhanaNLP's ghana-speech with 263 hours of Twi, AfriSpeech-200's Ghanaian-accented English, NaijaVoices, and Meta's MMS weights with their per-language adapters, which reach usable error rates from about four hours of fine-tuning data [V]. Two cautions remain. The University of Ghana licence also forbids derivatives, and a model fine-tuned on the corpus is arguably one, so such a model can be trained and used within the project but its weights should not be redistributed without the lab's written agreement; the lab's own work overlaps with this project, so ask rather than assume. And non-commercial status attaches to each use rather than to the project's intentions, so if a bank, a mobile-money operator or a lender later wished to deploy the model, or if anything were ever charged for, models trained on non-commercial data could not follow. The practical policy is to keep the provenance tiers the models repository already defines and, where it matters, to train two lines: a research line that uses everything available, and an open line trained only on CC-BY, CC0, Apache-licensed and self-collected data, whose weights can be released without restriction and handed to any partner. The stated purpose also strengthens ethics applications and funding applications, since LINGUA Africa and Lacuna Fund prefer open, non-commercial outputs, and it belongs on the app's consent screen, because traders' willingness to be recorded turns on who benefits.

## 5. Resources from elsewhere that transfer

Because the user asked for data regardless of geography, the study also inventoried resources that can carry acoustic, linguistic or task knowledge into a Ghanaian system. The most useful fall into five groups.

For acoustic pretraining and multilingual fine-tuning, the permissively licensed candidates are Omnilingual ASR itself, the w2v-BERT 2.0 encoder under MIT, and XEUS, whose unlabeled pretraining map verifiably includes Twi, Fante, Ga and Ewe sources through MMS-ulab-v2 [V]; XEUS is the only encoder confirmed to have heard Ga. Among neighbouring-language corpora, WaZoBiaSpeech offers about 2,500 hours of Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba and Nigerian Pidgin under CC-BY-4.0 with an unscripted portion [S], NaijaVoices offers 1,800 hours of the three Nigerian languages under a non-commercial licence [S], and Baoulé, an Akan-family language of Côte d'Ivoire, has 11 validated CC0 hours in Common Voice plus a WAXAL subset [V]/[S]. Tonal West African languages are the closest acoustic analogue to Akan, Ga and Ewe.

For the numeric and transactional task, no open corpus of shop or market speech exists in any language. The nearest relatives are the Gridspace-Stanford Harper Valley Bank corpus of simulated telephone banking calls with money amounts under CC-BY-4.0 [V], SpokenWOZ with price-range slots under a non-commercial licence [S], and the DSTC11 speech-aware MultiWOZ recipe, which shows how to turn a text dialogue set into speech by synthesising it and then having humans re-record it [V]. Timers and Such and Meta's STOP corpus supply the modelling formalisms for numeric slots and nested semantic parses. AudioMNIST (MIT) and the Free Spoken Digit Dataset (CC-BY-SA) are trivial but useful for a digit-spotting head [V].

For code-switching methodology, the Yoruba-English YECS corpus of roughly 120 hours with word-level language tags [S] is the annotation scheme to copy for Twi-English, and MUCS 2021 Hindi-English under CC-BY-SA-4.0 is the permissive testbed for mixed-tokenizer approaches [S].

For robustness to market noise, the single most relevant resource found is GESMA, a Ghanaian environmental soundscape dataset of about 62 hours recorded in situ on smartphones with a marketplace category, released under CC-BY [S]. Paired with the public-square and cafeteria channels of DEMAND and the far-field overlap of CHiME-6 and DiPCo, it gives a realistic augmentation bank without any recording.

For text, the eBible metadata confirms redistributable Biblica translations for Akuapem Twi, Asante Twi and Ewe and none for Ga, Fante or Dagbani [V]; AfriSenti and AfriQA provide colloquial Twi under CC-BY-4.0 [V]; and the World Food Programme's Ghana market price series on HDX supplies product names, local units and realistic cedi price ranges for synthesising sale utterances and for plausibility checks in extraction [S].

## 6. Creating the data that does not exist

The missing corpus is small and well defined: consented recordings of people stating sales, with the item, quantity, unit and price known at capture time. Published data-efficiency results bound the target. Adapter fine-tuning of MMS reaches usable error rates from about four hours of transcribed speech [V]; a 2025 benchmark across XLS-R, w2v-BERT, Whisper and MMS on African languages including Akan finds Whisper and MMS strongest between one and ten hours with early plateaus, while XLS-R and w2v-BERT keep improving to 50 and 100 hours [S]; and a Gronings study achieved a 25 percent relative error reduction from 24 minutes of real speech plus synthetic augmentation, and a further 20 percent from self-training on under three hours of unlabeled audio [S]. A reasonable plan is five to ten in-domain hours for a working prototype of the constrained task and 20 to 50 hours for robustness across markets [E].

Three tiers of recording give the best return per cedi. The first is scripted sale lines read by many speakers, which is cheap, feeds Common Voice if the sentences are CC0, and can be produced by writing several thousand Twi, Ga, Ewe and Pidgin sentences from the WFP product and price lists and having native speakers filter them. The second is role play between two paid speakers with a product and price card, which yields realistic bargaining, code-switching and numerals with the ground-truth tuple already known. The third is consented real transactions recorded push-to-talk by the trader at the moment of sale, confirmed on screen immediately, which doubles as the sale-extraction label and becomes the in-app data engine once the product exists. Metadata should record language, market, stall type, device, ambient level, pseudonymous speaker identity, sex and age band. Recording should use a mid-range Android phone with a wired lavalier microphone and keep parallel phone-microphone captures, since the deployed app will hear the latter. Karya's open-source Android recorder library and Label Studio cover capture and transcription tooling at no licence cost [V].

Costs in the region are documented. Kenyan academic projects paid transcribers about 38 US dollars per audio hour and reviewers 27 [S]; AfriSwitch paid annotators 10 to 50 dollars per hour [S]; a Ghanaian clinical trial compensated participants 40 cedis per day [S]. On those figures a 30-hour, three-language pilot with 300 speaker sessions, transcription with review, microphones and modest compute comes to roughly 3,500 to 5,000 US dollars [E]. Inter-annotator agreement should be measured by double-transcribing five to ten percent of clips and computing symmetric word error rate; published values run from about 3 percent for clean post-edited speech to 17 percent for code-switched speech [S], so a style guide for orthography, numerals and English spans must precede transcription.

The legal minimum in Ghana is registration with the Data Protection Commission as a data controller under the Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843), renewable every two years, with processing while unregistered an offence [S]; ethics approval from the University of Ghana Ethics Committee for the Humanities, KNUST's CHRPE or HuSSREC, or Ashesi's review process [S]; and written or witnessed oral consent in the participant's language. Voice is identifying, so recordings are personal data whatever the content. Continuous ambient capture in a public market is the weakest possible consent story because customers and neighbouring traders are recorded without agreement; push-to-talk capture with a stall notice, short clips, deletion of clips in which a third party is intelligible, and storage of derived ledger entries rather than raw audio are the defensible design. Releasing the corpus under CC-BY-4.0 satisfies funders' openness requirements while permitting commercial use.

Funding routes with a plausible fit are the LINGUA Africa programme run by Microsoft's AI for Good Lab with the Gates Foundation, Google.org and the Masakhane hub, whose data-creation track offered up to 50,000 dollars in cash and the same in compute and whose 2026 round closed on 15 June [S]; Lacuna Fund, which financed the Ashesi corpus and requires open licensing [S]; Masakhane community pilot grants announced at the Deep Learning Indaba [S]; and the GSMA Innovation Fund for Impactful AI, which funds mobile-delivered AI in low- and middle-income countries [S]. Partners who already hold speakers, tooling or data are GhanaNLP, Ashesi's CSIS with Nokwary, the University of Ghana HCI Lab, and the KNUST Responsible AI Lab; radio archives are a large source of conversational Twi but are copyrighted and their speakers never consented to machine-learning use, so they suit research evaluation only unless a station licenses them, as happened for Bambara in Mali [S].

### 6.1 Why separating voices out of online video is not a data strategy

A tempting shortcut is to take market footage from YouTube, isolate the traders' voices with a source-separation model such as Meta's SAM Audio, and transcribe the result. SAM Audio, released in December 2025, is a prompted separation model: given a noun phrase such as "woman speaking", a click on a person in the video, or a time span, it returns that sound and a residual. It is a diffusion transformer trained with flow matching on 48 kHz audio in clips capped at 30 seconds, comes in roughly 0.5, 1 and 3 billion parameter sizes, requires a GPU, and is distributed under Meta's SAM License, which permits derivative works and commercial use conditional on compliance with privacy and data-protection law [V]. Meta's own subjective scores place isolating one speaker among several as its weakest category, at 3.1 to 3.6 of 5, against about 4 for separating speech from non-speech [V]; a bargaining exchange is two overlapping voices in crowd babble, which is the hard case.

Two findings argue against using the separated audio as recogniser input. A March 2026 study fed SAM Audio output to five Whisper variants on noisy Bengali and English speech: signal quality rose by every objective measure, yet word error rose in every configuration, from about 11 to 22 percent for Whisper base on English and from 66 to 77 percent for Whisper large-v3 on Bengali [S]. Generative separation removes phonetic detail that recognisers depend on, and recognisers trained on natural noisy speech tolerate noise better than synthesis artefacts. Independently, the deployed app will hear raw phone microphones, so a model trained on cleaned speech would meet an acoustic distribution it has never seen. The defensible use is narrower: keep the raw audio as the training input and offer the separated track to human transcribers as a listening aid, then measure on twenty clips whether transcription becomes faster or more accurate. Large-scale in-the-wild pipelines such as Emilia-Pipe already chain cheaper components, namely UVR separation, pyannote diarisation, Silero voice activity detection and quality filtering, and they are built to yield single-speaker monologue, which is the opposite of the dialogue this project needs [V].

The stronger objection is provenance. YouTube's terms forbid downloading content except through means the service provides or with permission from YouTube and the rights holder, and the corpora that have survived scrutiny, YODAS and its code-switching derivative, restrict themselves to videos whose creators chose a Creative Commons licence and still operate in contested territory [S]. Ghanaian market vlogs and television price-check segments, which are the best-matching content because a reporter asks a trader a price in Twi and receives a number, are almost all standard-licence and copyrighted, and the traders in them never consented to machine-learning use; under Act 843 a voice is personal data, and the Act's treatment of research as a compatible further purpose is thin ground for training a model that will be shipped [S]. Yield compounds the problem: vlogs are narrated by the vlogger while trader speech is brief and off-microphone, so a usable fraction of five to ten percent of runtime would be optimistic [E], and every usable hour still costs the same transcription fee as an hour recorded with consent. What survives of the idea is to reserve Creative Commons footage found through the YouTube Data API for a held-out evaluation set, to seek written agreements with broadcasters where an archive would be transformative, as the Bambara Kunkado corpus did with four Malian radio stations, and to reproduce vlog acoustics by filming consented market sessions in which SAM Audio serves as a transcription aid over audio the project owns.

## 7. What architecture the evidence supports

Four channels were assessed: always-on on-device listening, a cloud voice agent driven by push-to-talk or WhatsApp voice notes, a telephone or USSD agent for feature phones, and passive capture of mobile-money transactions. The evidence favours a combination of the last three with a gated, constrained on-device recogniser, and it argues against the first as the product's core.

Always-on listening fails on three independent grounds. The acoustic ground is measured: Kejetia market averages 68 to 70 dBA in the morning and 71 to 77 dBA in the afternoon [S], which puts normal speech at a phone half a metre away near or below zero signal-to-noise ratio. The recognition ground is the 45 percent word error floor above. The platform ground is that Android 14 and later forbid starting a microphone foreground service from the background or at boot [S], and Transsion's HiOS and XOS power managers kill background services regardless, so the trader would need to re-open the app after every kill. Microsoft Research's Dukawalla field study in Nairobi, the only published field trial of voice business-data capture in Africa, reported exactly these problems together with privacy discomfort about speaking amounts aloud and social norms around announcing money; its participants valued the generated charts more than the voice channel [V].

A push-to-talk or voice-note design changes the acoustics because the phone is at the mouth, changes the consent story because the trader initiates every capture, and changes the recognition task because a short utterance with a closed item vocabulary and a dedicated number grammar is far easier than open transcription. Estimated cloud cost for a pipeline of speech recognition, a small language model with a strict output schema, and a spoken confirmation is one to four US dollars per user per month at twenty entries a day [E]; a user's own data cost for voice notes is negligible. WhatsApp's Cloud API delivers inbound voice notes free of charge, but from 1 October 2026 in-window service replies in the Rest of Africa region become billable after 1,000 free messages per business number per month [S], so confirmations should be batched. On-device, the same task can run through the sherpa-onnx Omnilingual 300M int8 model or a fine-tuned streaming zipformer with hotword boosting for numerals and product names, with Silero voice activity detection gating the decoder; the closest published device figure is a French streaming zipformer running on a Samsung A32 with a Helio G80 but with many errors [V], and no phone real-time factor is published for Omnilingual, so this must be measured on the reference device before it is promised.

Passive capture of mobile-money receipts is the highest-value component per unit of effort. Bank of Ghana figures put 2025 mobile-money value at 4.54 trillion cedis, up 51 percent after the electronic transfer levy was repealed in April 2025 [S], and a study of informal firms found half already using digital payment platforms for business [S]. Because most traders receive payments to personal wallets rather than merchant accounts, the practical route is reading the network's SMS receipt on the device, either through Google Play's declared exception for SMS-based financial transaction tracking or through a notification listener [S]. That gives exact amounts for the digital share of sales without any speech, leaving voice for cash sales and credit entries, which are the entries traders most want tracked.

For the extraction step, a 0.6-billion-parameter model with grammar-constrained decoding can fill three or four slots from a short in-domain utterance, but constrained decoding fixes format rather than meaning: a wrongly heard amount arrives well formed [V]. Numbers are over-represented in recognition errors, so the amount slot needs its own defence in the form of a number-spotting grammar, inverse text normalisation for Twi and Pidgin cedi forms including the surviving old-cedi habit of quoting "five thousand" for fifty pesewas [S], and explicit read-back with confirmation when confidence is low or the amount is implausible for the item. One hackathon system built for Nigerian traders gates every money token on per-word confidence and asks the user to type any amount below the threshold; the pattern is sound even though none of these prototypes has been evaluated in the field.

A telephone or USSD channel remains the only route to feature-phone users. Outbound voice broadcast in Ghana is priced around 0.15 cedis per minute by local aggregators [S], international CPaaS numbers cost far more, and a USSD short code requires an NCA numbering application through an aggregator [S]. This is a second-phase addition once a smartphone product has retention data.

### 7.1 Where the open interface is: the Android recognition service

A recurring question is whether a Ghanaian language can be contributed to "the speech recogniser most apps use". Two clarifications settle it. WebRTC, Google's open-source real-time communication stack, contains microphone capture, the Opus codec, echo cancellation, noise suppression and a small voice activity detector, and no speech recogniser at all; the detector this repository references works on signal energy and spectral shape and has no notion of language, so there is nothing in WebRTC to which Twi could be added. The recogniser behind the Android SpeechRecognizer API and behind Chrome's Web Speech API is Google's closed speech service, whose languages are added internally. Google's free endpoint already accepts codes for Akan, Ewe and Ga and transcribes Twi at 56 to 65 percent word error on the GhanaNLP benchmark [V]; improvement in that system arrives through corpora and partnerships, of which WAXAL with the University of Ghana is the recent example, rather than through code contributions.

What is open is the interface. Android allows any application to implement `android.speech.RecognitionService`; once installed and selected under Settings and Voice input it becomes the recogniser to which other applications' SpeechRecognizer calls are routed, with no change on their side, and open keyboards such as HeliBoard and AnySoftKeyboard honour that selection while Gboard and SwiftKey hard-wire their own engines [S]. Kõnele, an Estonian project distributed on F-Droid, has used exactly this route for over a decade to expose open-source recognisers to the whole phone through the standard intents [V]. The practical form of contributing Twi to the platform is therefore a Twi recognition service backed by sherpa-onnx and the fine-tuned model from section 3, which would give every compliant application Twi voice input, make the ledger app one consumer among many, and serve as an opt-in channel for the consented in-domain recordings section 6 calls for. A general dictation service at today's error rates would frustrate users; one scoped to numbers, prices and goods, and labelled as such, would not. For contributions to open projects rather than to Google, Mozilla Common Voice accepts CC0 sentences and recordings and already has Twi enabled, Omnilingual ASR is Apache-2.0 and documents a fine-tuning path for adding languages [V], the k2-fsa projects host community-trained models in their releases, and GhanaNLP's Khaya and benchmark are the Ghanaian home for both data and models.


### 7.2 Close-talk wearables, asynchronous structuring and decision models

Two product categories from outside the domain inform the design. Wearable recorders such as Plaud's Note and NotePin record a conversation from a card or a pin worn by the speaker, then transcribe it in the cloud with speaker labels, custom vocabulary and templated summaries produced by frontier language models, and the vendor claims 112 languages [S]. Little of that stack transfers directly: it is cloud-bound, its language claims are unverified for Twi, and it does nothing about the consent of the customer on the other side of the counter. What does transfer is the form factor and the timing. A microphone on the trader's lapel or a pin on the apron turns the acoustic problem from far-field to close-talk, and at the noise levels in section 8 that is the difference between usable and unusable audio. A device that captures short notes through the day and lets the trader confirm them at closing time separates capture from confirmation, which answers Dukawalla's finding that owners cannot stop to record while customers wait, at the cost of the immediate read-back that catches errors while memory is fresh; the two modes can coexist, and a wired lapel microphone costing a few dollars with the phone in a pocket delivers the acoustic benefit without a proprietary device.

Decision models are the second category. Jev, released by TypeSafe AI in September 2026, and Laya, an Apache-2.0 open-weight model of about 421 million parameters from Convai Innovations, take a state such as a transcript together with a typed question and a fixed set of allowed answers, and return a chosen option with a calibrated probability for every option in a single forward pass, in roughly 33 milliseconds for Laya on local hardware, without generating text; Laya's backbone is ModernBERT-large, it claims coverage of more than a hundred languages, and it needs about a gigabyte of memory [S]. That is the shape of the classification and gating steps in this pipeline: whether an utterance is a sale, a purchase or a credit entry, which product from a closed vocabulary was named, which unit applies, and whether the amount is confident enough to book or must be read back. The sikabook-models plan already assigns these steps to a fine-tuned MiniLM, which is the same design at a fraction of the size, and a calibrated confidence output is precisely what the confirmation threshold needs. Whether Laya's multilingual claim reaches Twi is untested, its memory footprint sits at the limit of a 3 to 4 GB phone, and any deployment would need fine-tuning on Ghanaian transcripts, so its sensible role is as a server-side or evaluation-side comparator against the on-device classifier rather than a replacement for it.

## 8. Users, markets and the evidence on record keeping

The informal sector employs about 69 percent of Ghana's 12 million workers on the 2023 household survey [S], and the 2024 establishment census counted 1.87 million establishments of which 92 percent are informal and micro-sized [S]. Literacy in the 2021 census is 69.8 percent nationally, 87.9 in Greater Accra and 32.8 in Savannah, with women below the national figure in 12 of 16 regions [S]. Twi is the market lingua franca in Accra as well as Kumasi, with Ga, Ewe, Hausa and Pidgin alongside; Dagbani and Hausa dominate in Tamale, Fante in Takoradi [S]. Twi-English code-switching is pervasive, and English numerals routinely sit inside Twi price frames [S]. Web-traffic shares in Ghana put Samsung around 26 percent and Transsion brands together around 33 percent, and unit sales are dominated by Tecno Spark and Pop, itel A-series and Infinix Smart and Hot lines with 2 to 4 GB of memory [S]; a gigabyte of data costs roughly 3 to 10 cedis depending on bundle [S].

The causal literature is unambiguous about what kind of record keeping helps. Rule-of-thumb training in the Dominican Republic, which taught separating business and personal cash and simple weekly notes, improved practices and revenues more than formal accounting instruction, especially for owners with weak baseline skills [S]. Ghanaian microenterprise trials by Fafchamps, McKenzie, Quinn and Woodruff and by Karlan, Knight and Udry found that capital and consulting produced gains that reverted within the study window [S]. Half of the micro-owners in a Central Region study kept no financial records, and the main reason given was fear of exposure to authorities [S]. A voice bookkeeper therefore competes on effort and trust: it must cost seconds rather than minutes per entry, speak the day's total back in Twi for the non-literate third of southern traders, keep data on the device by default, and state plainly that nothing goes to the revenue authority unless the trader sends it. Under the GRA's modified taxation scheme, turnover between 20,000 and 750,000 cedis is taxed at a flat 3 percent [S], which means a truthful sales ledger is the tax base and traders know it.

The product landscape confirms the diagnosis. No production voice ledger for informal traders with published adoption data exists anywhere. OZÉ in Ghana reached 16,000 active users with a typed English interface and became a lender; Kippa in Nigeria raised over 14 million dollars, shut its bookkeeping app in January 2024 and left users without their records; the bookkeeping products that survive do so as on-ramps to credit [S]. India's VoiceKhata is the only commercial voice-first ledger found and publishes no user numbers [S]. The gaps that nobody has filled are a field-validated numeric confirmation dialogue for low-literacy users, a voice ledger in any Ghanaian language, and any randomised comparison of voice against touch or paper on persistence and accuracy. A Ghana deployment that measured these would be producing first-of-its-kind evidence, which is a research contribution independent of commercial outcome.

## 9. A staged plan with gates

The first stage, which needs no new data, is to make the app truthful and measurable. Replace the simulated recogniser with sherpa-onnx, load the Omnilingual 300M int8 model and the Silero VAD, and measure real-time factor, memory and battery on a Tecno Spark-class device; remove or clearly label the mock speaker identifier; correct the licence matrix and the numeral glosses; and build the evaluation set the models repository already calls for, namely a few hundred self-recorded market utterances with ground-truth tuples, because read-speech error rates will flatter every model. In the same stage, prepare the Ashesi, WAXAL and Common Voice Dagbani data with the existing scripts and confirm the KasaSpeech licence in writing with the KNUST lab.

The second stage is the data pilot described in section 6, run in parallel with ethics and Data Protection Commission registration, which take four to eight weeks and should start immediately. Its gate is a fine-tuned model whose amount-slot accuracy on the self-recorded market set, after read-back and confirmation, exceeds 90 percent, with a sale-event F1 that beats the rules baseline by a margin the evaluation package can measure [E]. The third stage is a closed pilot with 30 to 50 traders recruited through market associations, susu collectors or mobile-money agents, using push-to-talk entry, mobile-money SMS ingestion and a spoken daily summary, with day-30 retention, entries per day, share of credit entries and confirmation-correction rate as the primary metrics. Only after that pilot should the team decide whether an ambient mode, a feature-phone channel or a credit-scoring export deserves investment.

## 10. Where the research stands in other languages and regions

The same research interest is being pursued elsewhere, and the field has a recognisable shape. At the top sit two quantified academic efforts and one rich field study. Beneath them sit a handful of commercial voice-finance products with disclosed scale, almost all of which place voice on top of a payment, lending or collections rail or a telephone line. Beneath those sits a very large wave of prototypes: a GitHub search for repositories named "voice ledger" created since January 2025 returns 94, with dozens more under "khata", "bookkeeping" and "traders", and none reports a user [V]. Across every language and region surveyed, no voice bookkeeper for informal traders has published adoption, retention, in-the-wild error rates or effects on credit access. The gap in the literature is evaluation rather than prototypes, and a Ghanaian deployment that measured these things would be the first of its kind.

The closest academic work is at the SnT centre of the University of Luxembourg, in the TruX group of Tegawendé Bissyandé and Jacques Klein, with Maimouna Ouattara and Abdoul Kader Kaboré. Their LoResLM 2025 position paper argued for conversational agents in Mooré so that informal traders in Burkina Faso could manage transactions independently, and reported no system [V]. Their LaTeLL 2026 paper, whose code release is public though anonymised, does exactly what this project intends: it extracts mobile-money debt-ledger entries, who owes whom, repayments and balance queries, from short spoken merchant utterances in Mooré, French and Mooré-French code-switched speech [V]. The design is instructive. Four unfused recognisers run in parallel, a w2v-BERT model for Mooré, Omnilingual ASR decoded as Mooré and as French, and MMS for French. Their outputs feed a deterministic analysis-by-synthesis amount recoverer built on a bidirectional Mooré numeral parser and generator with frozen rewrite rules for typical recognition drift; it re-synthesises candidate numerals, scores them against the noisy transcripts, assigns confidence tiers, and abstains and escalates to audio when uncertain. A guided language-model judge then receives the recovered amount as a hint and emits the JSON ledger. On a sealed real holdout scored exactly once the system reaches 76.0 percent amount accuracy on Mooré and 85.0 percent on code-switched speech, with direction accuracy above 90 percent in both regimes; there is no user study and the audio is withheld [V]. Two lessons transfer directly. The authors identify amount recovery, not general transcription, as the central difficulty, which matches the benchmark findings in section 3, and the deterministic numeral machinery that the sikabook-models rules already contain is half of their core module.

The only field deployment is Microsoft Research Africa's Dukawalla in Nairobi, whose manuscript was read directly. Seven small businesses each used a voice-entry prototype for two weeks between August and October 2023 after a week of ethnography; the recogniser's transcript was converted by a language model into a four-column sales record and turned into charts [V]. The study is qualitative and reports no accuracy or retention figures, but its findings are the best available account of why voice fails at a counter. Owners said they could not start recording while customers waited, customers asked why their purchases were being spoken aloud, one accountant refused the voice channel as strange, background chatter broke language identification, participants code-mixed Swahili, English and local dialects while the recogniser was monolingual, local product words went unrecognised, the colloquial currency word "bob" was filed as a quantity, and "one fifty" was ambiguous between a time, 150 shillings and 1.50 dollars [V]. Every one of these failure modes has a Twi analogue in the market.

Ghana already has a cluster of relevant actors. The University of Ghana HCI Lab benchmarked seven Akan recognisers across four corpora including spontaneous financial dialogues and found strong domain dependence, with an in-domain model reported near 10 percent word error on the Ashesi financial corpus against the roughly 42 percent that GhanaNLP's general model records on Akan [S]. Ashesi and Nokwary own the finance-domain corpus, and Nokwary runs Twi WhatsApp banking. GhanaNLP maintains the benchmark with its finance split, an open Twi recogniser and Twi voices. Mobobi's Abena AI is a shipped Twi assistant for airtime and transfers rather than a ledger. Kola Market, a GSMA Innovation Fund grantee, serves over 4,500 merchants, four fifths of them women, through WhatsApp, SMS and voice with embedded inventory finance [S]. Sikaflow, a bookkeeping and point-of-sale platform that went nationwide in June 2026, advertises an assistant in Twi, Ga, Hausa and Ewe without any published evaluation [S]. A hackathon entry called TraderWise takes Twi voice notes over WhatsApp into Whisper and a language model for decision support rather than record keeping, MTN Ghana's 2026 Tɛkyerɛma Pa hackathon produced Twi and Ewe voice entries for mobile money with a feature-phone IVR line, the University of Cape Coast has secured a grant for a 500-hour parallel corpus across Twi, Ga, Ewe, Dagbani and Fante, and the Ministry of Education announced in January 2026 that Twi, Ewe, Dagbani and Hausa would be integrated into AI tools with Google [V]/[S]. Nobody in this cluster has built or evaluated a voice ledger.

Elsewhere in Africa the nearest resources are Senegal's WolBanking77, a NeurIPS 2025 datasets-track release of 9,791 Wolof banking queries across 77 intents with over four hours of speech under CC-BY-4.0, motivated explicitly by Senegal's illiteracy rate and Wolof's spoken-not-written status and offering the template for a Twi equivalent [V]/[S]; Nigeria's Ghost Ledger, a WhatsApp tool in which traders type or speak market language into structured records and which a single press feature credits with over 1,500 Lagos micro-businesses by early 2026 [S]; a Kinyarwanda voice banking agent over ordinary phone calls launched by Pindo and I&M Bank in 2026 [S]; and the Tanzanian randomised trial by Innovations for Poverty Action and Viamo in which 1,200 micro-enterprise owners receive Kiswahili IVR lessons, a generative voice chatbot, both or neither, the only registered trial of a generative voice service for informal businesses, with results pending and with financial literacy rather than record keeping as the outcome [S]. In South Asia the durable findings are older and cautionary: Microsoft Research India found that in a mobile money task no low-literacy participant completed it by text, 72 percent did by spoken dialogue and all did with a graphical interface [S], and work at LUMS found a dialogue system with very high recognition accuracy still achieving only 38 percent task success [S]. The recent FormBharo study of a Hindi phone agent that fills enrolment forms for low-income mothers in rural Maharashtra concludes that models should be chosen by end-to-end completion rather than component accuracy [S]. Digital Green's Farmer.Chat team has published the best recipes for noisy field speech, including gated enhancement, target-speaker selection, weighted domain lexicons and a transcript quality gate, and reports Hindi at 16 percent and Odia at 35 percent word error on agricultural field recordings [S]. Commercially, Bangladesh's Hishab converts an ordinary GSM voice call into transaction records for shopkeepers without smartphones and launched with the operator Robi, India's VoiceKhata sells push-to-talk entry in twelve languages for 199 rupees a month, Brazil's Jota runs payments and bookkeeping by audio inside WhatsApp for a reported 150,000 customers, and India's Sarvam has placed some ten million local-language voice calls for a rural lender, on the lender's side for collections [S]. None of these has published an evaluation.

On the speech-technology side the frontier for spoken amounts in low-resource languages is thin and recent. The first published numeric error rate for African languages comes from Intron's AfriVox-v2 benchmark, where even the best regional model gets roughly one spoken number in five wrong [S]. Isolated digit recognition is solved at 98 percent in Bangla and Swahili, and continuous multi-word amounts remain unmeasured outside that single figure and the Ghanaian finance split in section 3 [S]. Fine-tuned regional or edge models beat frontier multimodal models everywhere they have been compared: WAXAL's fine-tuned edge models reach 38 percent macro word error against 65 percent for the best zero-shot system across 19 African languages, and Indian benchmarks rank regional models above GPT-4o and Gemini [S]. Around 20 hours of in-domain data changes outcomes, with Yoruba-English code-switched fine-tuning on 21 hours halving word error and Omnilingual's in-context mode moving unseen languages from 26 to 14 percent character error with ten examples [S]. Synthetic speech is cost-effective, with 250 real plus 250 synthetic Hausa hours matching 500 real [S]. Metrics must be entity-aware, since number normalisation alone shifts word error by more than two points and switch-aware metrics reverse system rankings [S], and Bangla studies find that Whisper mangles numeral rendering where CTC models stay literal [S]. Several pieces are simply absent: no inverse-text-normalisation grammar exists for Akan, Ewe or Ga numerals and currency although eleven Indic languages have them; no contextual-biasing or grammar-constrained decoding result exists for any African-language recogniser; no real-time factor has been published for any African-language model on a mid-range Android processor; no benchmark tests small language models on structured extraction from Twi, Swahili or Hausa transcripts under recognition noise; and Ga is absent from Omnilingual and from most multilingual encoders while Ghanaian Pidgin has no corpus at all [E].

Where a Ghanaian contribution fits follows from these gaps. A spoken-transaction corpus and numeric-error benchmark for Twi, Ga, Ewe and Pidgin, with word-level language tags, canonical written forms and entity-level metrics, would be the first such resource outside closed industrial data and belongs at LREC or the NeurIPS datasets track, with WolBanking77 as the model. Inverse-text-normalisation grammars for Akan, Ewe and Ga numerals and cedi and pesewa forms, with a study of how much apparent word error on existing corpora is formatting artefact, mirror the Polish result for an African language. First public baselines on KasaSpeech with switch-aware metrics, and a Twi adaptation of the Luxembourg amount-recovery design, would extend the only quantified pipeline to a second language family. Constrained decoding for amounts on a fine-tuned 300-million-parameter model with real-time factor, memory and battery measured on Tecno-class phones would fill the on-device gap, and a controlled study of small language models on record extraction under real recognition noise would fill the extraction gap. Above all, a pilot with thirty to fifty traders that reports retention, entries per day and confirmation-correction rates against observed sales would be the first quantitative field evaluation of a voice bookkeeper in any language. The natural venues are LaTeLL and LoResLM for the pipeline work, AfricaNLP, SIGUL, Interspeech and ICASSP for the speech work, and COMPASS, ICTD and CHI for the field study. The natural collaborators and reviewers are the SnT group in Luxembourg, the Dukawalla authors at Microsoft Research Africa and UC Irvine, the University of Ghana HCI Lab, the Ashesi and Nokwary dataset authors, GhanaNLP, the WolBanking77 authors at Gaston Berger University and Université Côte d'Azur, Digital Green's voice-pipeline team, and for an impact study the Tanzania trial team at Innovations for Poverty Action.
